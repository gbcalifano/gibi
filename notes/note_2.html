[2] Does AI-generated food look more appetizing than the real thing?

Generative AI can conjure a plate of food in seconds. Across two experiments, people spotted the fakes easily — yet often found the AI images more appetizing, at least until they were told which was which.

Posted on June 27, 2026 by Giovanbattista Califano

Open any food-delivery app, recipe site, or social feed and a growing share of the mouth-watering dishes you scroll past were never cooked. They were generated. Text-to-image models now produce photorealistic food on demand, and that imagery is quietly entering the channels where people decide what to eat.

That raises two practical questions for anyone who studies — or markets — food. First, can people tell the difference? And second, does it even matter for how appealing the food looks? The answers turn out to be more tangled than the headlines suggest.

Why this question matters

Food photography is persuasion. A more appetizing image lifts expected taste, willingness to order, and willingness to pay, long before anyone takes a bite. If a synthetic image can out-perform a real one on appeal, the economics of food imagery change — and so does the question of what a picture is implicitly promising the consumer.

To test this, real and AI-generated food images were compared across the full processing spectrum — from unprocessed foods (think raw produce) through processed to ultra-processed items.1 Two online experiments separated two different things people are usually asked to do at once: spotting a fake, and liking an image.

Can people even tell?

In the first experiment, participants simply judged whether each image was real or AI-generated. They were good at it — strikingly so. Detection was easiest for ultra-processed foods, where generative models tend to over-deliver: surfaces too glossy, geometry too regular, a kind of uncanny perfection that real food rarely has. For simpler, unprocessed foods the task was harder, because there is less for the model to over-render.

The short version

People are not easily fooled. The "tell" is over-perfection, and it is loudest in complex, ultra-processed dishes.

The twist: the fakes often win

Detectable is not the same as unappealing. When the source of the image was not disclosed, the AI-generated images were often preferred — rated as more visually appealing than the genuine photographs. The same over-perfection that gives a fake away can also make it look better, at least at a glance.

So we have a paradox: viewers can tell an image is probably synthetic, and still find it more attractive than the real thing. Appeal and authenticity are pulling in different directions.

What a label does

The second experiment added one ingredient: disclosure. Participants were told the origin of each image before rating its appeal. That single piece of information moved the ratings.

Telling people an image was a genuine photograph significantly boosted its appeal. Telling them an image was AI-generated dampened that boost. The picture on screen never changed — only the label did — and yet the same image was judged differently depending on what viewers believed about how it was made.

Why disclosure flips it

The likeliest explanation is authenticity. A glossy, hyper-real image is attractive on its own terms, but "real food" carries a value that a known synthetic cannot — it implies the dish exists, that it can actually be ordered, cooked, or eaten. Learning that an image is genuine adds that authenticity premium; learning it is AI-generated strips it away and can even invite suspicion.

This fits a broader pattern in how people relate to AI in the kitchen: appeal and trust are separate currencies. People can admire what an AI produces while remaining wary of relying on it — a tension that also shows up when consumers judge AI "chefs" and AI-assisted cooking.2

What it means

For marketers, AI imagery is a sharp tool with a fragile edge. It can make food look superb, but the advantage leans on the viewer not knowing — and detection is easy and disclosure is moving from optional to expected. An undisclosed synthetic hero shot is a reputational and regulatory liability waiting to surface.

For platforms and regulators, the disclosure effect is the actionable finding: a label is not cosmetic, it materially changes how appealing — and how trustworthy — an image is judged to be. That makes provenance labelling a lever worth designing carefully.

For researchers, the results are a caution about using generative AI as a stand-in for real stimuli or even for human respondents. If a model's output systematically looks "too good," treating it as equivalent to the real thing — or as a synthetic "silicon sample" of participants — can quietly bias what you measure.3

Frequently asked questions

Can people tell AI-generated food images from real photos?

Yes. In controlled experiments people identified AI-generated food images easily — and they were most accurate for ultra-processed foods, where AI tends to render an unrealistically flawless, glossy look. Detection was harder for simpler, unprocessed foods.

Do AI-generated food images look more appetizing than real ones?

When the source is not disclosed, AI-generated food images were often preferred to real photographs on visual appeal. The advantage is not absolute, however: it depends on whether viewers are told how the image was made.

Does labelling an image as AI-generated change how appealing it looks?

Yes. Disclosing that an image is a genuine photograph significantly boosted its appeal, whereas revealing that an image was AI-generated reduced that boost. The label itself shifts perception, even when the picture stays the same.

What does this mean for food marketing?

AI-generated imagery can look highly appetizing, but its persuasive edge is fragile. Once consumers know an image is synthetic, its appeal drops — so undisclosed AI imagery carries reputational and regulatory risk, and transparent labelling is likely to matter for trust as AI images spread through digital food marketing.
